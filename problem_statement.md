## Problem Overview
Space suits are essential life-support systems for astronauts during Extra-Vehicular Activities (EVAs), but current suit stiffness and mass significantly restrict natural human motion. Routine tasks on Earth (tool use, carrying loads, stabilizing posture) become high-effort activities in a pressurized suit, increasing physical strain and operational risk.

As lunar operations expand toward longer and more demanding missions, these limitations can affect both immediate crew safety and long-term astronaut health.

## Key EVA Risks

### 1) Elevated metabolic load and CO2-related risk
<strong>Primary contributors:</strong>

<ul>
  <li>Transfer operations (moving from point A to point B)</li>
  <li>Post-fall recovery maneuvers</li>
  <li>Bracing and bearing body+suit mass/inertia during work</li>
</ul>

Astronauts must generate high torques to overcome suit pressure and mass while performing mission tasks, which drives exertion and can contribute to CO2 management challenges inside a confined suit environment.

### 2) Fall risk during suited locomotion and tasking
<strong>Primary contributors:</strong>

<ul>
  <li>Gait instability</li>
  <li>Limited range of motion</li>
  <li>Slipping in external terrain/worksite conditions</li>
</ul>

Suit-constrained mobility can make loss-of-balance and recovery more difficult, creating scenarios where a single fall can lead to serious mission and safety consequences.

## Problem Gap
Current EVA operations still face persistent human-performance and safety risks linked to suit-induced mobility and exertion constraints. Existing mitigations are limited, and stronger, validated countermeasures are needed to reduce both acute incident risk and cumulative physical burden.

## One-Sentence Problem Statement
How can we reduce exertion- and mobility-driven safety risks in suited EVA operations without compromising mission capability?
